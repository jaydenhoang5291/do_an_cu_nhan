\subsection{System Architecture}
The proposed evaluation platform is organized as a measurement-driven handover
analysis chain with two complementary subsystems. The radio simulation
subsystem produces radio-measurement traces from a controlled mobility and
propagation scenario. The handover-emulation subsystem then replays those
measurements in StormSIM to execute and observe protocol-level handover behavior
through a 5G Core environment.

The radio simulation subsystem is responsible for the measurement side of the
study. It defines the simulation area, places gNBs according to the selected
cell layout, updates UE positions according to the mobility model described
later in this chapter, evaluates the propagation model, and records the radio
state at each simulation step. The recorded state includes UE position and
height, serving-cell information, LOS/NLOS condition, path loss, shadow fading,
received power, RSRP, SINR, and neighbor-cell measurement context. This
subsystem therefore answers the radio-level question: under the configured
scenario, when do the measurements suggest that another gNB should become the
serving cell?

The handover-emulation subsystem is responsible for the protocol side of the
study. StormSIM emulates the UE and gNB behavior needed for the selected
handover scenarios and interacts with the 5G Core through the corresponding
control-plane and user-plane interfaces. Before handover evaluation, the UE is
registered and a data session is established. After that, the measurement trace
is replayed as the input that determines handover opportunities. For each
handover trial, the emulator applies the configured transport conditions,
executes the selected handover procedure, observes timer behavior, and records
the resulting success or failure and delay.

\subsection{Radio Simulation Design}

\subsubsection{Network Layout}

The radio simulation subsystem is designed to generate structured radio-measurement traces from a controllable geometric scenario. The simulation area entered by the user is represented as a rectangular study region placed at the center of a 10 km by 10 km canvas. This separation between the full canvas and the active study rectangle makes it possible to draw the cellular layout around the region while keeping the UE movement bounded inside the selected area.

Ground gNBs are deployed on a flat-top hexagonal grid. The geometric hexagon radius is \(R=500\) m, so the distance between adjacent gNB centers is approximately \(\sqrt{3}R\), or about 866 m. This value is not entered manually for every gNB. Instead, the simulator generates enough candidate hexagonal sites to cover the user-defined rectangle and a surrounding buffer. As a result, the number of gNBs automatically follows the size of the selected study area.

The network layout is later combined with the UE position, height, direction, and speed supplied by the mobility model in Section~\ref{sec:ue-mobility-model} to compute the UE--gNB geometry and radio measurements.

The gNB placement procedure follows the axial-coordinate representation for hexagonal grids. Each hexagon is stored as \((q,r)\), and the third cube coordinate is recovered as

\begin{equation}
    s=-q-r.
\end{equation}

This keeps the cube-coordinate constraint \(q+r+s=0\) while storing only two coordinates, which is a standard practical approach for hexagonal-grid algorithms \cite{redblob_hexagons}. The flat-top axial-to-Cartesian conversion used for the gNB layout is

\begin{align}
    x &= R\left(\frac{3}{2}q\right),\\
    y &= R\left(\sqrt{3}r+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}q\right),
\end{align}

where \(R\) is the geometric hexagon radius. These coordinates are shifted by the canvas center so that the generated grid is centered on the simulation map.

To adapt the topology to an arbitrary user-defined rectangle, the algorithm first estimates the axial coordinate ranges needed to cover the requested width and height:

\begin{align}
    q &\in \left[
    \mathrm{int}\left(-\frac{W}{1.5R}\right)-1,\,
    \mathrm{int}\left(\frac{W}{1.5R}\right)+1
    \right],\\
    r &\in \left[
    \mathrm{int}\left(-\frac{H}{\sqrt{3}R}\right)-1,\,
    \mathrm{int}\left(\frac{H}{\sqrt{3}R}\right)+1
    \right],
\end{align}

where \(W\) and \(H\) are the user-entered simulation width and height, and \(\mathrm{int}(\cdot)\) denotes integer conversion. The additional margin of one axial step avoids missing edge cells after the skewed axial grid is converted into Cartesian coordinates. The simulator then enumerates all \((q,r)\) pairs in these ranges, computes \(s=-q-r\), converts the hex center to Cartesian coordinates, and keeps only the centers that fall inside the simulation rectangle expanded by a boundary buffer:

\begin{equation}
    b=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}R.
\end{equation}

Thus a candidate gNB is retained when

\begin{equation}
    x_{\min}-b \le x \le x_{\max}+b,
    \qquad
    y_{\min}-b \le y \le y_{\max}+b.
\end{equation}

This design makes the gNB count a consequence of the requested study area instead of a fixed scenario constant. A larger rectangle automatically produces more hexagonal sites, while a smaller rectangle keeps only the sites needed near that region. The buffer also ensures that UEs near the simulation boundary still observe nearby external cells, reducing artificial edge effects in RSRP, SINR, and serving-cell selection.

\subsubsection{Radio Parameters}

The radio parameters define the fixed link-budget and measurement settings used
by the radio simulator. These values are separated from scenario variables such
as UE height and mobility state. In the implemented workflow, UE height is an
input selected within the research range, while the remaining radio parameters
provide a common baseline for received-power, RSRP, SINR, and serving-cell
selection calculations.

The carrier frequency, gNB transmit power, and antenna gains are used in the
link-budget calculation after path loss and shadow fading are evaluated. The
system bandwidth and receiver noise figure determine the thermal-noise level
used in the SINR calculation. The fixed gNB antenna height and the selected UE
height define the three-dimensional UE--gNB geometry and the propagation height
regime. The UE height is therefore not treated as a single default scenario
constant; it is varied within the configured 1.5--300 m range for terrestrial,
transition, and aerial cases.

The RSRP value used in this thesis is a system-level approximation derived from
the received downlink power. For a 10 MHz carrier, the implementation assumes
50 resource blocks based on the E-UTRA transmission-bandwidth configuration
and 12 subcarriers per resource block when mapping received power to RSRP
\cite{3gpp_36104,3gpp_36211}. This abstraction is sufficient for generating
interpretable measurement traces, but it is not intended to reproduce a full
physical-layer CRS-RSRP, SS-RSRP, or CSI-RSRP measurement implementation
\cite{3gpp_36214,3gpp_38215}. The 3 dB margin is then
used by the RSRP-based serving-cell rule described later in this chapter.

\subsubsection{Propagation-Model Calculation Flow}

The propagation model is executed for each UE--gNB candidate link at every simulation step. Its role is to transform the current geometry and UE height into radio fields that are later used for serving-cell selection, interference calculation, SINR calculation, and dataset generation. The model follows the theoretical chain introduced in Chapter 2, but here the focus is on how the chain is organized inside the simulator workflow.

The simulator does not rely on a separate terrestrial-versus-aerial mode switch. Instead, the UE height is the controlling input for the propagation regime. A low-height UE naturally falls into the UMa height range, while elevated UEs are evaluated by the UMa-AV expressions when their height enters the aerial range. This design keeps the radio calculation consistent across all altitude cases: the same processing chain is used, and only the height-dependent model selection changes.

This flow is applied to candidate serving cells and to the first-tier interfering cells used in the SINR calculation. For each UE--gNB link, the sampled LOS/NLOS state and the current shadow-fading value are stored in a per-link cache. The shadow-fading value is reused while the UE movement accumulated for that link remains below 25 m, and a new value is sampled after the update distance is reached. This keeps the large-scale fading variation spatially smoother than an independently sampled value at every time step.

For a selected gNB, received power and RSRP are computed as

\begin{align}
P_{\mathrm{rx}} &= P_{\mathrm{tx}}+G_{\mathrm{tx}}+G_{\mathrm{rx}}-PL-SF,\\
\mathrm{RSRP} &= P_{\mathrm{rx}}-10\log_{10}(50\times12).
\end{align}

The SINR calculation uses the serving-link received power as the desired signal. Interference is calculated from the six nearest non-serving gNBs around the serving gNB, which approximate the first tier of co-channel interferers in the hexagonal layout. Thermal noise is then added to this interference term before converting the result to dB. This design makes SINR more sensitive than RSRP to aerial scenarios, because a higher UE can observe stronger received power from both the serving gNB and neighboring gNBs. The neighbor-cell RSRP fields are therefore not only auxiliary logging fields; they provide the radio context needed to explain why a UE may experience high serving-cell power while its SINR remains limited by interference.

\subsubsection{Handover Decision and Output}

In 3GPP RRC measurement reporting, Event A3 is used when a neighbouring cell
becomes better than the serving cell by a configured offset, so it provides a
measurement-level condition for identifying candidate serving-cell changes
\cite{3gpp_38331}. The radio simulator applies this idea as an RSRP-based
serving-cell rule. At initialization, the strongest candidate cell is selected
as the serving gNB. After that, the current serving cell is kept unless a
candidate cell exceeds the current serving-cell RSRP by the configured handover
margin. In the implemented radio trace generator, candidate \(c\) replaces
serving cell \(s\) only when

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RSRP}_{c}>\mathrm{RSRP}_{s}+M_{\mathrm{HO}},
\qquad M_{\mathrm{HO}}=3~\mathrm{dB}.
\end{equation}

Thus, the recorded handover flag marks the simulation step where the exported
serving-cell index changes according to this A3-like RSRP condition. The Python
radio simulator does not apply time-to-trigger, measurement filtering, or A5
threshold checks. A5-style threshold conditions are considered in the
CHO-capable StormSIM workflow when the measurement trace is replayed for
protocol-level handover evaluation.

The exported dataset records UE position, height, direction, LOS probability,
LOS/NLOS state, path loss, shadow fading, RSRP, received power, SINR, speed,
serving gNB index, handover flag, and six neighbor-cell RSRP measurements. These
fields are later used to inspect how UE movement and altitude affect the radio
conditions around serving-cell changes.

\subsection{UE Mobility Model}
\label{sec:ue-mobility-model}

This section describes the UE mobility model used by the radio simulator. Two mobility concepts are discussed: Random Walk and Manhattan mobility. Random Walk is included as a reference model because it is a common baseline for random movement, while Manhattan mobility is the model actually used in the implemented simulation scenario. This distinction is important because the thesis studies urban-style movement where UE trajectories are constrained by road geometry rather than freely changing direction in an open plane.

The Random Walk model is an unconstrained mobility model in which the UE changes its movement direction randomly over time \cite{bai2003}. It is introduced only as a reference mobility concept because it allows free movement in a two-dimensional plane without road constraints. Since the implemented scenario represents urban-style movement, the simulator uses the Manhattan mobility model instead.

The Manhattan mobility model is a geographic-restriction mobility model designed to represent movement on an urban street grid \cite{bai2003}. The service area is divided into horizontal and vertical road segments that form an orthogonal grid. A UE moves only along these road segments and may travel in one of four directions: East, North, West, or South. Direction changes are permitted at intersections, where the UE may continue forward or select another valid road while remaining inside the service area.

In the implemented simulator, the road grid is constructed from vertical and horizontal line sets over the rectangular study area. The distance between adjacent road lines is entered as a simulation parameter. Each UE begins at one of the generated road intersections, so its initial position already satisfies the Manhattan-grid constraint. At an intersection, the available movement directions are determined by the remaining road segments inside the rectangle. This prevents the UE from selecting a direction that would immediately leave the study area.

UE movement is continuous along each road segment. During one simulation interval, the nominal travel distance is determined by the base UE speed and the duration of the time step:

\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{move}} = v \Delta t,
\end{equation}

where \(d_{\mathrm{move}}\) is the nominal distance traveled during one time step, \(v\) is the base UE speed, and \(\Delta t\) is the time-step duration. The base speed is sampled once for each UE from the height-dependent speed interval selected for the scenario height. Therefore, UEs in the same simulation run share the same altitude but may have different sampled speeds. This is useful for multi-UE simulations because the dataset can contain several independent trajectories under the same altitude condition.

The effective movement distance can be reduced by the stop-and-go mechanism. Let \(\alpha_k\) denote the speed factor at time step \(k\), where \(0\le\alpha_k\le1\). The distance applied to the UE position update is

\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{eff}}(k)=\alpha_k v\Delta t.
\end{equation}

When the UE is moving normally, \(\alpha_k=1\). When the UE is stopped, \(\alpha_k=0\). During the acceleration phase after a stop, \(\alpha_k\) increases gradually until the UE returns to its base speed. In the current simulation setting, after three direction changes the UE pauses for nine steps and then ramps up over two steps. This mechanism is not a full traffic-flow model, but it prevents the mobility trace from becoming an unrealistically constant-speed sequence and creates more varied radio-measurement behavior near intersections.

For a movement direction \(u_k\in\{\mathrm{E},\mathrm{N},\mathrm{W},\mathrm{S}\}\), the position update follows the road axis rather than an arbitrary angle:

\begin{equation}
    (x_{k+1},y_{k+1}) =
    \begin{cases}
    (x_k+d_{\mathrm{eff}}(k),y_k), & u_k=\mathrm{E},\\
    (x_k,y_k+d_{\mathrm{eff}}(k)), & u_k=\mathrm{N},\\
    (x_k-d_{\mathrm{eff}}(k),y_k), & u_k=\mathrm{W},\\
    (x_k,y_k-d_{\mathrm{eff}}(k)), & u_k=\mathrm{S}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

When the remaining distance in the current time step is larger than the distance to the next intersection, the UE first reaches that intersection, selects a valid outgoing road, and then continues moving with the remaining distance. This detail is important because it allows one simulation step to cross an intersection without losing distance, while still enforcing the Manhattan-grid constraint.

The Manhattan update procedure can be summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item initialize each UE at a random road-grid intersection;
    \item assign an initial valid direction from the available cardinal directions;
    \item compute the effective travel distance for the current step;
    \item move the UE along the current road segment until the distance is exhausted or an intersection is reached;
    \item at an intersection, randomly select one valid direction and update the turn counter if the direction changes;
    \item trigger a pause and ramp-up cycle after the configured number of direction changes.
\end{enumerate}

This mobility model is especially relevant to the radio-measurement objective of the thesis. As the UE moves along the street grid, its distance and relative direction to surrounding gNBs change over time. These changes affect LOS probability, path loss, shadow fading, RSRP, interference, and SINR. When a neighboring gNB becomes stronger than the current serving gNB by more than the handover margin, the serving-cell index changes and a handover flag is recorded in the dataset. Thus, the combination of the hexagonal gNB layout and Manhattan mobility provides a consistent geometric foundation for studying radio-measurement variation and HO-related serving-cell behavior.

StormSIM is used to execute the protocol-level part of the handover study. In
the preceding radio simulator, a serving-cell change only indicates that the
radio measurements prefer another gNB. In StormSIM, the same change is replayed
as a handover opportunity so that signaling, timers, transport impairment, and
completion delay can be observed. This separation follows the NG-RAN mobility
view in which handover includes target preparation, UE reconfiguration or
execution, path switching, and completion or failure handling, not only radio
target selection \cite{3gpp_38300,3gpp_38331,3gpp_38413}.

measurement-driven handover flow. Before the handover experiment starts,
StormSIM connects to the 5G Core, registers the UE, and establishes a PDU
session. Packet loss is temporarily disabled during this preparation phase so
that registration and session establishment do not dominate the handover
results. After the UE is ready, the configured packet loss, latency, and jitter
values are restored and applied to the virtual radio link and Xn path.

\subsubsection{Measurement-Driven Trigger}

The measurement input used by StormSIM contains \texttt{Step},
\texttt{connected\_gnb}, neighbor-cell RSRP columns named
\texttt{gnbX\_rsrp}, and an optional \texttt{Type} field. The initial serving
gNB is selected from the strongest RSRP value in the first measurement row. In
normal measurement mode, StormSIM does not recompute the target cell from RSRP.
Instead, it treats a change in \texttt{connected\_gnb} between two consecutive
rows as the handover trigger. The previous serving gNB becomes the source, the
new \texttt{connected\_gnb} becomes the target, and the \texttt{Type} field
selects Xn or N2 handover. If the field is missing, Xn handover is used.

This design makes StormSIM a protocol emulator for an already generated
measurement trace. The RSRP values remain useful as radio context and, in CHO
mode, as the input used to build RRC-style measurement reports. In this mode,
StormSIM smooths RSRP using a nearest-three-step window, updates the UE
measurement store, and sends a \texttt{MeasurementReport} from the UE to the
source gNB. The CHO policy then evaluates A3 and A5 conditions. A3 is satisfied
when a neighbor is better than the serving cell by the configured offset and
hysteresis, while A5 is satisfied when the serving cell is below a threshold and
the neighbor is above a threshold \cite{3gpp_38331}. The implemented default
CHO parameters are a 5 dB A3 offset, 0 dB hysteresis, and -100 dBm serving and
neighbor thresholds for A5.

\subsubsection{Xn, N2, and CHO Execution}

For Xn handover, the source gNB creates a target-side virtual radio connection,
starts \texttt{TXnRELOCprep} and \texttt{TXnRELOCoverall}, and forwards the UE
context to the target gNB. The context-forwarding message is retried up to
three times with a short delay between attempts. If the forwarding step fails,
StormSIM records a transport-failure event and the handover attempt is later
reported as failed. If the target preparation succeeds, the source sends a
handover command to the UE. The UE starts T304, executes the target connection,
switches its serving gNB when the execution succeeds, and stops T304. After the
target gNB obtains the UE context, it performs path switching toward the core
network; the source-side Xn relocation timers are stopped when the successful
execution and path-switch stages are observed.

For N2 handover, the source gNB sends an NGAP \texttt{HandoverRequired}
message toward the AMF and starts \texttt{TNGRELOCprep} and
\texttt{TNGRELOCoverall}. The target preparation, handover command, and path
switch are then handled through the NGAP procedure between the gNBs and the
5G Core \cite{3gpp_38413}. In the thesis workflow, the \texttt{Type} field in
the measurement trace selects whether the triggered attempt follows the Xn or
N2 branch.

StormSIM also includes a CHO-capable execution path. In this path, the source
gNB prepares candidate target gNBs and sends a \texttt{CHOConfig} to the UE.
The UE evaluates the stored candidate conditions every 500 ms using the latest
RSRP values. When the condition is satisfied, the UE triggers execution toward
the selected candidate. This is different from normal measurement mode: normal
mode replays serving-cell changes that already exist in the trace, while CHO
mode uses measurement reports and candidate conditions before executing the
handover.

\subsubsection{Timer and Monitor Output}

The handover monitor records the high-level procedure state as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NULL}\rightarrow\mathrm{PREPARE}\rightarrow\mathrm{EXECUTE}
\rightarrow\mathrm{COMPLETE}\rightarrow\{\mathrm{SUCCESS},\mathrm{FAIL}\}.
\end{equation}

separate preparation failure, execution failure, recovery behavior, and overall
procedure completion.

The timer values in Table~\ref{tab:stormsim-handover-timers} are the default values used by the current StormSIM source, not parameters read from the YAML scenario file. Most of them are hard-coded as timer constants in the timer package. T304 is defined as a 1000 ms default variable and is changed only if the measurement scenario explicitly overrides the \texttt{T304DurationMs} field at startup.

StormSIM exports three main handover-related outputs. The
\texttt{timer-events-*.csv} file stores timer start, stop, timeout, cancel,
restart, and transport-failure events. The
\texttt{handover-trial-summary-*.csv} file stores one row per handover attempt,
including trial identifier, source gNB, target gNB, handover type, final
result, failure reason, and total handover time. The \texttt{ho-time-log.csv}
file stores timestamps for the monitor phases and is used to inspect the order
of preparation, execution, and completion events.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}

The primary metrics are handover success rate, failure rate, and handover
completion time. For \(N_{\mathrm{HO}}\) attempted handovers and
\(N_{\mathrm{succ}}\) successful handovers, the success rate is

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{succ}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{succ}}}{N_{\mathrm{HO}}}\times100\%.
\end{equation}

For successful attempts, the delay is measured from the moment StormSIM starts
the handover procedure to the moment the target gNB reports successful
completion. The reported delay statistics include mean, median, and
95th-percentile delay. Failed attempts are analyzed using the failure reason
and timer events, such as Xn transport failure, preparation timeout, T304
timeout, or recovery-timer behavior.

Two additional mobility-quality indicators can be derived from the same logs.
A ping-pong handover is counted when the UE moves from gNB \(A\) to gNB \(B\)
and then returns to \(A\) within a short observation window. This metric is
important because frequent back-and-forth handovers indicate unstable target
selection near cell boundaries. Radio-link-failure-related behavior is
identified from T310, T311, T301, and T312 timer events and from failed trials
that do not complete before the configured timeout. In the exported logs, the
column named \texttt{pdr} is populated from the configured packet-loss value;
therefore, it is interpreted in this thesis as loss probability rather than as
packet delivery ratio.
